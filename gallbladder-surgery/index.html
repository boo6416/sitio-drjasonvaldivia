What are the symptoms of gallstones?

The gallbladder is a small sac under the liver that stores bile. When stones form and one of them blocks the outlet, biliary colic begins. This is what most often brings people in:

  • Pain under the right ribs or in the pit of the stomach, which builds fairly quickly and then stays steady.
  • The pain comes on after eating, especially fatty food, and often at night.
  • It lasts from twenty minutes to several hours and then settles completely until the next episode.
  • It spreads to the back, to the right shoulder blade or to the shoulder on that side.
  • Nausea and vomiting along with the pain.
  • Bloating, belching or heaviness after meals, which are less specific and can have other causes.
  • Many people have stones and never know: they turn up on an ultrasound done for another reason.

When is surgery recommended, and when is it not?

The general rule is simple and worth saying plainly: stones that cause symptoms are treated by removing the gallbladder, and stones that have never caused symptoms are usually left alone and watched. There is no medication that reliably dissolves them and no diet that removes them; eating carefully may space the episodes out, but it does not remove the stones nor prevent one of them from blocking the outlet one day. Surgery is recommended once there has been one or more episodes of biliary colic, because they are likely to recur and because each episode can become complicated: inflammation of the gallbladder, a stone passing into the bile duct, obstruction with jaundice, or inflammation of the pancreas. There are situations where surgery is considered even without symptoms — polyps above a certain size, very large stones, a gallbladder with a calcified wall, or some blood disorders — and that is decided case by case, not by rule. Before scheduling, your history and the necessary tests are reviewed; if there is any suspicion that a stone has passed into the bile duct, the order of things changes and that is explained in consultation.

How is the operation done?

The gallbladder is removed whole: the stones cannot be taken out on their own, because a diseased gallbladder forms them again. In the great majority of cases this is done laparoscopically and you go home the same day or the next.

  1. The history of your episodes is reviewed, your abdomen is examined, and the diagnosis is confirmed with ultrasound. Depending on the case, blood tests and liver function tests are requested, and sometimes additional imaging of the bile duct.
  2. A laparoscopic cholecystectomy is done under general anaesthesia. This is decided together with the anaesthetist at the pre-operative visit, where your history and the risks of anaesthesia itself are reviewed.
  3. Access is through four incisions between half a centimetre and one centimetre: the camera goes through one and the instruments through the others. The abdomen is inflated with gas to create space and visibility.
  4. Before anything is divided, the cystic duct and artery are clearly identified; that step is what defines the safety of the operation. The gallbladder is then separated from the liver and removed through one of the incisions.
  5. The procedure usually takes between 45 and 90 minutes. A few hours of observation follow; most people go home the same day and some need to stay overnight.

When to go to the emergency room: if the pain does not settle after several hours, if fever or chills appear, if your eyes or skin turn yellow, if your urine darkens or your stools turn pale, or if vomiting stops you keeping anything down. That can mean the gallbladder has become inflamed, that a stone has passed into the bile duct, or that the pancreas is inflamed, and none of the three waits for an appointment.

Recovery, going back to work, and risks

For the first two or three days there is mild to moderate pain, controlled with ordinary painkillers; shoulder discomfort from the gas used during surgery is also common and settles on its own. Walking from the same day is part of the treatment. Going back to work depends on what the job demands: desk work is usually resumed in five to seven days, and work involving lifting or sustained physical effort at around two to four weeks. These are reference ranges adjusted at your follow-up visits, not a guaranteed timetable. About food: no special lifelong diet is needed. Light meals are advised for the first few days and then you return gradually to your usual diet; some people notice that for weeks or months they tolerate fatty food less well or have looser stools, and that usually improves with time. Like any operation it carries risks: wound infection, bleeding, bile leak, a stone left in the bile duct that has to be removed in a second procedure, and injury to the bile duct, which is uncommon but is the serious complication of this operation and the reason the step of identifying the structures is done so carefully. To these are added the risks of anaesthesia and, less often, injury to bowel or blood vessels, clots in the legs, and breathing or heart complications. There is also the possibility of converting to open surgery during the procedure: that is not a complication but a safety decision when inflammation or anatomy do not allow a clear view. This is not a complete list, and they do not carry the same weight for everyone: the ones that apply to you are reviewed before you sign the consent form, together with the warning signs that should make you call.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you live without a gallbladder?

Yes. The gallbladder stores bile, it does not produce it: the liver goes on making it and it passes straight into the intestine. Most people eat normally and live normally after recovery. Some notice for a time that they tolerate very fatty meals less well or have looser stools, and that generally improves over the months.

Can you just remove the stones and leave the gallbladder?

That is not what is done. A gallbladder that has formed stones forms them again, so removing only the stones leaves the problem in place. There is also no medication that reliably dissolves them and no diet that removes them.

When can I go back to work?

It depends on what your job demands. Desk work is usually resumed in five to seven days; work involving lifting or sustained physical effort, between two and four weeks. Those are general reference ranges, adjusted at your follow-up visits.

They found stones on an ultrasound but I've never had pain. Do I need surgery?

Usually not. Stones that have never caused symptoms are watched rather than operated on, because most people in that situation never run into trouble. There are exceptions that are assessed for surgery, such as certain polyps, very large stones, or a gallbladder with a calcified wall. What matters is that you leave the consultation knowing which symptoms should bring you back.

Does it hurt much afterwards?

Most people describe mild to moderate pain for the first two or three days, controlled with ordinary painkillers, plus shoulder discomfort from the gas used in surgery that settles on its own. Pain that increases instead of easing, or that comes with fever or yellowing of the eyes, is not part of the expected course and should be reported.

Is it always possible laparoscopically?

In the great majority of cases, yes. When inflammation is significant, there have been previous operations, or the anatomy is not clearly visible, it may be necessary to convert to open surgery during the procedure. That is not a complication: it is the correct decision when the work cannot be done safely, and it is better known beforehand than during.

This is scheduled surgery, not something to decide on the day you arrive: it needs a consultation, the tests the case calls for, the operation, and at least one follow-up visit before travelling.
