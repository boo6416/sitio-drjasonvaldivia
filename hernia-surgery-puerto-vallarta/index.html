What are the symptoms of a hernia?

A hernia is fat or bowel pushing through a weak point in the abdominal wall. The most common are inguinal, in the groin; umbilical, at the navel; and incisional, which appears over the scar of a previous operation. This is what most often brings people in:

  • A lump in the groin, at the navel or over a scar, anywhere from the size of a pea to the size of a lemon.
  • The lump is more visible when standing, coughing, straining or lifting, and shrinks or disappears when lying down.
  • A dragging, burning or heavy feeling in the area, especially at the end of the day or after exertion.
  • Discomfort when getting out of bed, climbing stairs or making an effort.
  • With an inguinal hernia, discomfort that can run down toward the groin or scrotum on the same side.
  • In many people it does not hurt at all: the lump is found on a routine exam or on a scan done for another reason.
  • Sudden severe pain with a firm lump that no longer goes back in: that does not wait for an appointment.

When is surgery recommended, and when can it wait?

In an adult the defect in the wall does not close on its own, and there is no medication, truss or exercise that repairs it: a truss holds the bulge and makes it more bearable, but it does not close the opening. Repair is recommended when the hernia hurts, has been growing, limits daily activity, or has been trapped at any point. For an inguinal hernia with little or no discomfort in a man, watchful waiting is an accepted and reasonable option, as long as you leave the consultation knowing exactly which warning signs mean going to the emergency room; it is worth knowing that over the years a substantial share of people who choose to watch end up having surgery anyway because the hernia grows or starts to bother them, so watching is a decision that gets reviewed, not a final answer. In women the balance leans earlier toward repair, because a femoral hernia, more common in women and sometimes hard to tell apart from an inguinal one on examination, becomes trapped more easily. Before scheduling, the conditions that raise pressure inside the abdomen are reviewed, because they affect the result: weight, chronic cough, constipation, difficulty passing urine, a planned pregnancy; sometimes those are worth addressing first. Your medical history is reviewed as well, because it weighs on the decision as much as the hernia itself.

How is the repair done?

The technique is chosen according to the type and size of the hernia, whether it is a first repair or a recurrence, and your own circumstances. It is scheduled surgery and in most cases you go home the same day.

  1. You are examined standing and lying down to see whether the lump reduces, the hernia is identified as inguinal, femoral, umbilical or incisional, and the defect is measured. When the findings are unclear, there have been previous operations or there is significant excess weight, an ultrasound or CT scan is used.
  2. Depending on the type of hernia and the approach, the procedure can be done under general, regional or local anaesthesia with sedation. This is decided together with the anaesthetist at the pre-operative visit, where your history and the risks of anaesthesia itself are reviewed.
  3. An open repair is done through one incision over the hernia; a laparoscopic repair, through three small ports. Both are valid options for an inguinal hernia and the choice depends on the case: laparoscopy is considered mainly when the hernia is on both sides, when it is recurrent, or when returning sooner to demanding physical activity matters.
  4. In adults, repair with mesh is the usual recommendation, because closure with sutures alone recurs more often. The mesh becomes integrated into the wall and, under normal circumstances, is neither removed nor needs special care. It is not free of drawbacks either, and those are explained before deciding.
  5. The procedure usually takes between 45 and 90 minutes. A few hours of observation follow and, in most cases, you are discharged the same day; some people need to stay longer.

When to go to the emergency room: if the lump becomes firm and can no longer be pushed back in, if there is sudden severe pain, if the skin over it turns red or purple, or if it comes with nausea, vomiting, fever, or you stop passing gas and stool. That can mean the bowel is trapped and losing its blood supply. Do not wait for an appointment and do not try to force it back in.

Recovery, going back to work, and risks

For the first few days there is discomfort when coughing, laughing and getting out of bed, managed with the painkillers you are given; walking from that same day is part of the treatment, not something to avoid. Going back to work depends above all on what the job demands: desk work is usually resumed in one to two weeks, and work involving lifting, long hours of driving or sustained physical effort at around four weeks, sometimes longer. These are reference ranges adjusted at your follow-up visits, not a guaranteed timetable, and a medical leave certificate is extended according to how each case progresses. It is common for a firmness or swelling to remain under the scar from inflammation and fluid; it usually settles on its own, sometimes in weeks and sometimes longer. Like any operation, hernia repair carries risks: wound infection, a collection of fluid or blood, pain that lasts longer than expected, numbness or discomfort in the area from nerve irritation, and recurrence of the hernia, which can happen even after a well-performed repair. To these are added the risks of anaesthesia and, less often, injury to bowel, bladder, blood vessels or nerves, clots in the legs, and breathing or heart complications; with a laparoscopic repair there is also the possibility of having to convert to open surgery during the procedure. This is not a complete list, and they do not carry the same weight for everyone: the ones that apply to you, given your age and your medical history, are reviewed before you sign the consent form, together with the warning signs that should make you call.

Frequently Asked Questions

I have a lump in my groin — is it a hernia?

A lump in the groin that appears when you cough, strain or stand, and reduces when you lie down, is usually an inguinal hernia. Not every lump there is one: lymph nodes, cysts and other causes exist too, so an examination is needed to confirm it. If the lump becomes firm, very painful and no longer reduces, that is an emergency.

When can I go back to work?

It depends on what your job demands, not on the calendar. Desk work is usually resumed in one to two weeks; work involving lifting, long hours of driving or sustained physical effort, at around four weeks and in some cases longer. Those are general reference ranges, adjusted at your follow-up visits.

Is mesh always used?

In adults it is the usual recommendation, because closure with sutures alone recurs more often. There are situations where a different decision is made, and that is discussed in consultation. Mesh also has drawbacks: it makes fluid collection under the wound somewhat more common and, uncommonly, it can become infected or be associated with persistent pain, situations that can require further surgery to remove it.

What happens if I don't have surgery?

The hernia does not close on its own and tends to grow over time, which makes the repair more complex later. The risk of the bowel becoming trapped in any given year is low, but published figures vary from study to study and none of them describes an individual case, so you will not be handed a percentage as if it were your prognosis. What is worth being clear about is that the risk does not go away with time, and that emergency surgery carries more risk than a planned operation.

Does it hurt much afterwards?

Most people describe mild to moderate pain in the first few days, controlled with ordinary painkillers and easing steadily. Coughing, laughing and getting out of bed are usually what bothers most. Pain that increases instead of easing is not part of the expected course and should be reported.

Can the hernia come back?

Yes, it can happen, even after a well-performed repair with mesh. It is less common with current techniques, but the risk is never zero and it increases with smoking, excess weight, chronic cough and early straining. That is why the recovery instructions are not a formality.
