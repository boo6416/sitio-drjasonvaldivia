What does an inguinal hernia feel like?

An inguinal hernia appears in the inguinal canal, a naturally weak point in the groin. It is almost always noticed before it hurts. This is what most often brings people in:

  • A lump in the groin, on one side or both, anywhere from the size of a pea to the size of a lemon.
  • It is more visible when standing, coughing, straining or lifting, and shrinks or disappears when lying down.
  • A dragging, burning or heavy feeling in the groin, especially at the end of the day or after exertion.
  • Discomfort running down toward the groin or, in men, toward the scrotum on the same side.
  • In men the lump can descend into the scrotum and make it look uneven.
  • Discomfort getting out of bed, climbing stairs or making an effort.
  • In many people it does not hurt: the lump is found on a routine examination.
  • Sudden severe pain with a firm lump that no longer goes back in: that does not wait for an appointment.

Does it always need surgery?

Not always, and it is worth saying clearly because it is the question most people arrive with. In a man with an inguinal hernia that causes little or no discomfort, watchful waiting is an accepted option: it is reviewed periodically and repaired if symptoms begin. That decision is only safe if you leave the consultation knowing exactly which warning signs mean going to the emergency room. It is also worth knowing that over the years a substantial share of people who choose to watch end up having surgery anyway, because the hernia grows or starts to bother them; watching is a decision that gets reviewed, not a final answer. Repair is recommended when the hernia hurts, has been growing, limits work or exercise, descends into the scrotum, or has been trapped at any point. In women the balance leans earlier toward surgery, because a femoral hernia is more common in them — it looks similar from the outside and is not the same underneath — and it becomes trapped more easily. Before scheduling, the conditions that raise pressure inside the abdomen are reviewed, because they affect the result: weight, smoking, chronic cough, constipation, difficulty passing urine. Addressing those first is sometimes worthwhile, and it is not a formality: it changes the risk of the hernia coming back.

How is it repaired?

In adults the repair reinforces the wall with a mesh, either through an open incision or laparoscopically. Both are valid options and the choice depends on the case, not on fashion. It is scheduled surgery and in most cases you go home the same day.

  1. You are examined standing and lying down, and both sides are checked — it is not unusual to find a hernia on the other side that had gone unnoticed. The hernia is identified as inguinal or femoral. When findings are unclear, an ultrasound or CT scan is used.
  2. An open repair can be done under regional anaesthesia or local anaesthesia with sedation; a laparoscopic repair requires general anaesthesia. This is decided together with the anaesthetist at the pre-operative visit, reviewing your history and the risks of anaesthesia itself.
  3. In an open repair, access is through one incision in the groin and the mesh is placed over the defect. In a laparoscopic repair the work is done behind the wall, through three small ports. Laparoscopy is considered mainly when the hernia is on both sides, when it is recurrent, or when returning sooner to demanding physical activity matters; open repair remains an excellent option and in some cases is the right one.
  4. The mesh reinforces the weakened area and becomes integrated into the wall; under normal circumstances it is neither removed nor needs special care. It also has drawbacks, explained before deciding: fluid collection and, uncommonly, infection or persistent pain.
  5. The procedure usually takes between 45 and 90 minutes. A few hours of observation follow and, in most cases, you are discharged the same day.

When to go to the emergency room: if the lump becomes firm and can no longer be pushed back in, if there is sudden severe pain in the groin, if the skin over it turns red or purple, or if it comes with nausea, vomiting, fever, or you stop passing gas and stool. That can mean the bowel is trapped and losing its blood supply. Do not wait for an appointment and do not try to force it back in.

Recovery, going back to work, and risks

For the first few days there is discomfort when coughing, laughing and getting out of bed, managed with the painkillers you are given; walking from that same day is part of the treatment. The area is often firm or bruised for a couple of weeks, and in men some scrotal swelling is common; it usually settles on its own and is checked at follow-up. Going back to work depends on what the job demands: desk work is usually resumed in one to two weeks, and work involving lifting, long hours of driving or sustained physical effort at around four weeks, sometimes longer. Impact exercise and weight training are resumed gradually, generally around four to six weeks. These are reference ranges adjusted at your follow-up visits, not guaranteed timetables. Like any operation it carries risks: wound infection, a collection of fluid or blood, and recurrence of the hernia, which can happen even after a well-performed repair. Specific to this area are groin pain that lasts longer than expected and numbness of the skin of the groin or thigh from nerve irritation; most improve with time and a minority need further treatment. Less often there can be swelling of the testicle on the operated side and, rarely, injury to the vas deferens or the testicular vessels. To these are added the risks of anaesthesia, clots in the legs, and breathing or heart complications; with the laparoscopic approach there is also the possibility of converting to open surgery during the procedure. This is not a complete list, and they do not carry the same weight for everyone: the ones that apply to you are reviewed before you sign the consent form, together with the warning signs that should make you call.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need surgery if it doesn't hurt?

Not necessarily. In a man with an inguinal hernia causing little or no discomfort, watchful waiting is an accepted option, as long as you know which warning signs mean going to the emergency room and you come back for review. Over the years a substantial share end up having surgery because the hernia grows or starts to bother them. In women the recommendation leans earlier toward repair.

When can I go back to work?

It depends on what your job demands, not on the calendar. Desk work is usually resumed in one to two weeks; work involving lifting, long hours of driving or sustained physical effort, at around four weeks and in some cases longer. It is adjusted at your follow-up visits.

When can I go back to the gym or lift weights?

Walking resumes the same day. Impact exercise and weight training are resumed gradually, generally around four to six weeks, and the return to maximum load is decided in consultation according to how healing is going. Going back too early is one of the things that increases the risk of recurrence.

Does the surgery affect fertility or sex life?

Sexual activity is resumed when discomfort allows, usually in two to three weeks, and a repaired hernia does not change sexual function. The vas deferens and the testicular vessels run through the area being operated on, so injury to those structures is a known although uncommon risk; it is reviewed before you sign the consent form, particularly if future fertility matters to you.

Can both hernias be repaired on the same day?

Often yes, and when the hernia is on both sides the laparoscopic approach allows both to be repaired in the same procedure without extra incisions. Whether it suits your case is decided at the consultation, considering the size of each side and your medical history.

What happens if I never have it repaired?

The hernia does not close on its own and tends to grow, which makes repair more complex over time. The risk of the bowel becoming trapped in any given year is low, but published figures vary from study to study and none describes an individual case, so you will not be handed a percentage as if it were your prognosis. What is worth being clear about is that the risk does not go away with time, and that emergency surgery carries more risk than a planned operation.

It is scheduled surgery: it needs a consultation, any tests the case calls for, the operation, and at least one follow-up visit before travelling. Flying too soon after a hernia repair is not advisable, and how long to wait depends on how your recovery goes. If you are planning a trip around this, write before booking flights so the timeline can be set out for your case.
